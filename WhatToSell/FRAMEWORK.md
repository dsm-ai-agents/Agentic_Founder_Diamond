# Founder Commercial Strategy Framework

Use this framework to teach founders how to turn an idea into a sellable, testable business.

## 1. Start with the customer

**What it is:** Define the specific person or company you want to serve first.

**What to teach:** Industry, company size, role, geography, buying power, and the situation they are in.

**Why it matters:** “Everyone” is not a market. A narrow first customer profile makes messaging, outreach, pricing, and product decisions much clearer.

---

## 2. Find the painful problem

**What it is:** Identify the problem that costs the customer time, money, revenue, reputation, or peace of mind.

**What to teach:** Ask what is frustrating, expensive, risky, slow, manual, or preventing growth today.

**Why it matters:** Customers pay to solve urgent problems—not for interesting features. The more painful and time-sensitive the problem, the easier it is to sell.

---

## 3. Identify the buying trigger

**What it is:** Understand what causes a customer to look for a solution right now.

**What to teach:** Look for events such as rapid growth, missed targets, compliance changes, hiring, churn, a system failure, new funding, or a new strategic priority.

**Why it matters:** A customer may have a real problem but still not be ready to buy. Buying triggers help founders contact people at the moment of highest urgency.

---

## 4. Create an outcome-led offer

**What it is:** Package the startup around a valuable result, not simply a product or feature.

**What to teach:** Use this formula: “We help [customer] achieve [valuable outcome] without [major pain or current limitation].”

**Why it matters:** Customers buy progress. An offer centered on an outcome is easier to understand, compare, and justify internally.

---

## 5. Define the first sellable version

**What it is:** Decide what can be sold before the full product is built.

**What to teach:** Consider a paid pilot, audit, implementation service, consulting engagement, workshop, concierge service, or prototype.

**Why it matters:** Early-stage founders need learning and revenue before perfection. Selling a narrow first version tests whether the problem and offer are genuinely valuable.

---

## 6. Price around value

**What it is:** Set a price that reflects the outcome, urgency, and economic value created.

**What to teach:** Compare the price with the cost of the problem, the cost of current alternatives, and the value of the result. Test pilot, setup, subscription, annual, and usage-based models.

**Why it matters:** Pricing reveals whether customers truly care. It also determines whether the business can become sustainable.

---

## 7. Position against alternatives

**What it is:** Explain why a customer should choose this solution over competitors, internal workarounds, agencies, spreadsheets, or doing nothing.

**What to teach:** Identify the current alternative, its weaknesses, and the one advantage your startup can credibly own.

**Why it matters:** Customers do not evaluate startups in a vacuum. Clear positioning makes the decision feel safer and more obvious.

---

## 8. Choose reachable channels

**What it is:** Select the fastest ways to get in front of ideal customers.

**What to teach:** Start with channels where the customer already spends time: founder networks, LinkedIn, email, niche communities, partnerships, events, content, or warm introductions.

**Why it matters:** A great offer fails if the right people never see it. Early on, founders should choose channels that enable direct learning, not just reach.

---

## 9. Build founder-led outreach

**What it is:** Personally start conversations with potential customers.

**What to teach:** Lead with a relevant observation or pain point, ask for insight rather than immediately pitching, and invite qualified prospects to a short conversation or pilot.

**Why it matters:** Founder-led sales produces the fastest feedback on messaging, objections, pricing, and customer needs. It is the shortest path to early revenue.

---

## 10. Use content to earn trust

**What it is:** Create useful, specific content around customer problems, insights, and outcomes.

**What to teach:** Share opinions, frameworks, mistakes to avoid, customer lessons, industry changes, and practical tools—not generic product promotion.

**Why it matters:** Content builds credibility before a sales conversation. It makes cold outreach warmer and creates an asset that compounds over time.

---

## 11. Validate with evidence

**What it is:** Measure customer behavior, not just positive feedback.

**What to teach:** Look for introductions, repeat meetings, pilot commitments, contracts, deposits, and payments. Ask direct questions about urgency, budget, decision-making, and alternatives.

**Why it matters:** Compliments do not validate a business. A willingness to commit time, money, or reputation is much stronger evidence.

---

## 12. Focus, learn, and iterate

**What it is:** Choose one customer segment, one problem, one offer, and one or two channels long enough to learn.

**What to teach:** Review feedback weekly. Improve the message, offer, price, and target customer based on real conversations and sales results.

**Why it matters:** Most early-stage startups fail through diffusion: too many customer types, features, channels, and ideas. Focus creates faster learning and clearer momentum.

---

## The teaching flow

Customer → Pain → Trigger → Offer → First sellable version → Price → Positioning → Channels → Outreach → Content → Validation → Iteration

Founders do not need to guess their way to product-market fit. They need a focused customer, a painful problem, a compelling offer, direct conversations, and repeated evidence that customers will pay.
